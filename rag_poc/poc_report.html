Proof of Concept: RAG System for Internal Document Search and Question Answering

DATASCI 267 — Generative AI — Assignment 5 Dawei David Lin | ddlin@berkeley.edu | April 2026

Executive Summary

We built a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system for internal question answering, serving both engineering and marketing teams with persona-specific responses grounded in a curated knowledge base of 48 ML/AI documents. The system demonstrates clear knowledge-based retrieval when relevant content exists and appropriate refusal when the knowledge base does not contain the requested information. We evaluated using three complementary metrics — BLEU (lexical overlap), sentence-embedding cosine similarity (semantic match), and LLM-as-judge (coherence and completeness) — across 75 gold-labeled validation questions and 30 held-out blind test questions. The best configuration (chunk_size=512, chunk_overlap=32, k=8, multi-qa-mpnet-base-dot-v1 encoder) achieved BLEU=0.078, semantic similarity=0.782, and judge=4.01/5.0 for the engineering persona, with zero hallucinations and correct out-of-domain refusal on the blind test. Our key limitation is that the LLM-as-judge exhibits a refusal-penalty bias that inflates scores on verbose, ungrounded answers — the other two metrics clearly capture this issue, and we recommend using all three in production monitoring. We recommend proceeding to pilot deployment with multi-metric monitoring; if HIPAA compliance permits, a pay-per-use API approach (~$8/day vs ~$200/day self-hosted) is preferred because it enables transferable prompts to leverage more advanced models as they become available, with relatively low switching cost. Self-hosting remains the fallback for stricter privacy compliance requirements, at higher hardware cost.

1. Introduction

We adapted the assignment's tech company scenario to our organization's life sciences context, maintaining the core dual-audience structure (technical + non-technical) while incorporating domain-specific regulatory constraints. Our company has approximately 1,000 employees, including roughly 100 engineers, 100 clinical diagnostics staff, and a marketing team. The VP of Engineering, with buy-in from the CMO, has sponsored a four-week proof-of-concept to evaluate whether a RAG system could improve internal document search and question answering for two distinct user groups: a technical audience needing precise, implementation-level detail, and a non-technical audience needing accessible, jargon-free explanations.

The POC system indexes 48 documents (32 arXiv papers, 4 Wikipedia topics, 8 blog posts) focused on ML/AI and Generative AI. It uses multi-qa-mpnet-base-dot-v1 for embedding, Qdrant as the vector database, and Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501 as the generator via vLLM (TP=4 across four L40S GPUs). Two persona-specific prompts direct the same retrieved context toward different audiences. The best configuration — chunk_size=512, chunk_overlap=32, k=8 — was selected from a 49-configuration sweep on 75 gold-labeled validation questions. We evaluate using three complementary metrics (BLEU, semantic similarity, LLM-as-judge) because no single metric captures every failure mode.

2. Key Findings

Finding 1: Three-metric evaluation reveals metric disagreement as a diagnostic signal

BLEU, semantic similarity, and the LLM-as-judge often disagree — and the disagreements are informative. On Q134, BLEU scored 0.016 (near zero) while the judge awarded a perfect 5, because the marketing response paraphrased the gold answer in simpler language. On Q0, all three converged (BLEU=0.858, sim=0.978, judge=5) because the response matched the gold nearly word for word. This demonstrates why all three are necessary: BLEU for lexical fidelity, similarity for paraphrase detection, and the judge for holistic quality.

Finding 2: Grounding constraints are the most important design decision — and the judge can't tell

Removing the grounding constraint ("base your answer ONLY on the context below") from the engineering prompt caused BLEU to drop from 0.088 to 0.050 (−43%), while the judge scored the ungrounded variant HIGHER (4.16 vs 3.44, +21%). The same inversion appeared on the marketing side: wrong-audience tone scored judge=4.40 vs baseline 3.40, despite BLEU collapsing 81%. This reveals the LLM-as-judge's RLHF helpfulness bias — it rewards confident-sounding responses and penalizes correct refusals. Figure 1 (left panel) visualizes this inversion: the blue bars (BLEU) drop on degraded prompts while the red bars (judge) rise. Relying on the judge alone would silently approve ungrounded answers; production monitoring must include BLEU or similarity checks alongside the judge.

Finding 4: Out-of-domain refusal works — the system knows its boundaries

Q225 (Thanos/Deadpool — pop culture question against an ML/AI corpus) and Q117 (mammalian brain — biology) were correctly refused by both personas. On the 30-question blind test, we qualitatively evaluated each response by reading the full text and categorizing as plausible, weak, refusal, or hallucinated. Figure 2 shows the results:

  • 80% plausible overall (83% engineering, 77% marketing)
  • 0 hallucinations across all 60 responses
  • ~12% appropriate refusals on out-of-domain or thin-context questions
Figure 2: Blind test response quality — 30 held-out questions, best config.

As Figure 2 highlights, zero hallucinations is the most important result — the grounding constraint successfully prevents fabrication. With only 3/30 questions clearly out-of-domain, refusal reliability needs broader validation before production deployment.

Finding 5: Standard reranking did not improve quality

A cross-encoder reranker (ms-marco-TinyBERT-L-2-v2) rescoring top-25 to top-8 produced neutral-to-negative results (judge −0.10 on both personas). The likely causes: our QA-optimized bi-encoder already captures relevance well, and the cross-encoder's web-search training domain mismatches our academic ML corpus. Reranking adds complexity without measurable gain for this setup.

3. Methodology

We swept 49 configurations across five dimensions: chunk_size (50–5,000), chunk_overlap (0–96), k (1–15), embedding model (5 variants), and prompt design (6 templates including 2 intentionally degraded). Each dimension was swept independently to build intuition, with a targeted chunk_size × chunk_overlap joint matrix for the two most impactful parameters. All sweeps ran on 75 validation questions × 2 personas (~4,545 question-runs, ~3,675 judge calls, ~2.9 hours on 4× L40S GPUs).

Metric validation used a bidirectional degradation test: each baseline prompt was compared against a deliberately degraded variant. BLEU reliably detected both degradations (−43%, −81%); semantic similarity detected weakly (−3% each); the judge inverted (+21%, +29%). The best config was validated on a 30-question held-out blind test (80% plausible, 0% hallucinated) via manual qualitative bucketing of all 60 responses.

4. Results and Findings — Challenges and Limitations

What works well. 80% plausible response rate on blind test with zero hallucinations (see Figure 2). Two-persona design serves both audiences from shared infrastructure. Out-of-domain refusal works on tested questions.

Key limitations:

  • LLM-as-judge reliability. The judge's refusal-penalty bias inflates scores for confident, verbose responses and penalizes correct refusals (see Figure 1, left panel). Production monitoring must include BLEU/similarity or human spot-checks.
  • Static corpus. No refresh mechanism. Production deployment needs scheduled re-ingestion.
  • Limited OOD validation. Only 3/30 blind test questions were out-of-domain. Broader testing needed.
  • Chunk size tradeoff. chunk=512/ov=32 is a compromise (see Tables 1–2). Multi-hop queries might benefit from larger chunks at 3.5× the cost.
  • Refusal quality varies with prompt format. In a separate blind test run using different prompt formatting, the Q225 marketing refusal cited actor names found incidentally in the retrieved arXiv context and misattributed them as relevant to the question. The notebook's current configuration produces a clean refusal without this issue, suggesting that prompt formatting affects refusal quality and should be monitored.

Recommendation. Proceed to pilot with the current best config (Table 2). Monitoring should include automated BLEU and similarity tracking alongside the judge, with monthly human spot-checks. At 1,600 queries/day, pay-per-use API (~$8/day) is 25× cheaper than self-hosting (~$200/day). If HIPAA requires on-premise data handling, self-host; otherwise, the API enables transferable prompts and lower switching cost when stronger models become available.
